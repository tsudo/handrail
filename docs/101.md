# Handrail 101

A ten-minute introduction to deciding which AI tools your organization can use, on what terms, and who decides. No security background needed.

## The problem

Somebody at your organization is already using AI on real work: a chat assistant, a notetaker in your video calls, an AI feature that appeared in software you already pay for. Most organizations respond with either nothing or a policy nobody reads. Neither tells you whether the notetaker is fine and the chat assistant is not.

Three Surfaces gives you one way to look at any AI use, decide how risky it is, and send the decision to the right person.

## Three questions about every AI use

Every use of AI touches three surfaces. Ask one question about each.

| Surface | The question | What makes it riskier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data** | What does it touch, and what happens to it? | Client or customer information goes in; the vendor keeps it or trains on it; your contract doesn't cover it |
| **People** | Who sees the output, and how much do they trust it? | It reaches clients or the public; people act on it without checking; a mistake affects someone's money, job or health |
| **Capability** | What is it allowed to do on its own? | It sends, changes or deletes things; it reads content from outside; it acts without a person approving |

## One rule

**The worst answer decides.** Rate each question Low, Medium, High or Severe. The overall rating is the worst one, not the average. A tool that is harmless in every way except that the vendor trains on your client files is a Severe tool.

One combination is always Severe: a tool that holds sensitive information, reads content from outside, and can send things out. Those three together are how AI tools get tricked into leaking data. Break any one of them and the rating comes back down.

## What the rating decides

| Rating | What usually happens |
| --- | --- |
| Low | Allow it |
| Medium | Allow it with conditions, such as "no client names in prompts" |
| High | Someone senior decides, in writing |
| Severe | Not allowed unless the owner makes a written, time-limited exception. Regulated data the organization already handles under its own program, such as health data under HIPAA, can be approved without an exception when a fixed set of safeguards is in place |

## Three levels

You do as much as your situation calls for.

| Level | For | You end up with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Know and decide | Small teams. A five-person consulting practice is the example | A one-page policy, a list of your AI tools with a rating for each, a check of each vendor's terms, and a card for when something goes wrong. Add client disclosure wording if AI output reaches clients. About four hours for four tools, with two record-only vendor questions finished within 30 days |
| 2. Route and control | Growing companies, or when customers start asking | An intake form, named owners, a risk register, controls for each tool, a review calendar, impact assessments for higher-risk tools |
| 3. Prove it | When you need to show an auditor, a regulator or an ISO 42001 assessor | A management system that ties every AI tool to its risks, controls and evidence |

Most small organizations stay at Level 1, and that is the right answer for them.

## Your first hour

1. **List every AI tool anyone uses for work.** Include AI features inside software you already have, and personal accounts used for work.
2. **Answer the quick questions for each one.** Seven questions; the worst answer is the rating.
3. **Name one owner** who makes the call on each tool.
4. **Check each vendor's terms** for two things: do they train on your data, and how long do they keep it.
5. **Write down what is allowed, what needs asking first, and what is not allowed.** That is your policy.

## Words you will see

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Use case | One tool used for one purpose. The same tool used two ways is two use cases |
| Ceiling | A limit you set in advance, such as "no client data in AI without written approval". Crossing it makes the use Severe |
| Trigger | A fact that adds a document, such as "AI output goes to clients" adding disclosure language |
| Impact assessment | A short write-up of who could be harmed if the tool is wrong, and what you do about it |
| Provisional | A rating that rests on an answer nobody has confirmed yet |
