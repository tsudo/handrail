# You have a policy, not a program

Most AI governance starts and stops with a policy. Someone writes down what people may and may not do with AI, it gets approved, and it goes into a shared folder. Meanwhile a meeting notetaker gets switched on in the video tool everyone uses, a team signs up for a writing assistant on personal accounts, and a vendor adds an AI feature to the CRM that is on by default. None of it went past the policy, because the policy has no way of seeing it.

A policy says what should happen. A program makes it happen. Four things separate them.

## 1. An inventory

A list of every AI tool that touches your organization's information, including AI features inside software you already use and personal accounts used for work. You cannot decide about tools you don't know exist. Building the list is usually the first surprise: most organizations find more AI in use than anyone thought.

## 2. A named owner

A person, by name, who decides whether a tool is allowed and on what terms. "The committee" or "IT" is not an owner. At a five-person firm the owner is probably the founder; at a larger company there are owners at each risk tier.

## 3. Decisions that happen

A request gets an answer in a known time, and the answer is written down with any conditions. If asking takes weeks, people stop asking. Handrail sets a decision target for each tier, from two working days for low-risk tools to thirty for a Severe exception.

## 4. A plan for when it goes wrong

Who to call, how to switch a tool off, and who decides whether a client needs to be told. At Level 1 this is a one-page card; at Level 2 it is a runbook.

## Where to start

Build the inventory first. List every AI tool anyone uses for work and who approved it, if anyone did. Then score each one with the [quick-score worksheet](../kit/quick-score-worksheet.md) and give it an owner and a decision. That is most of [Level 1](level-1.md).
